## 3. The Escalation Limiter — corrected yesterday, and today it moved again

**Yesterday I retracted my Aug 10 claim that energy infrastructure was being mutually spared.** It was not: Ras Laffan, Pearl GTL, Mina Al-Ahmadi, Mina Abdullah and Haifa were all struck in March, with $58bn in Gulf energy damage and 17% of Qatar's LNG offline for three to five years. That correction stands.

**What is actually limiting escalation, restated:** not a taboo on energy targets, but an economic constraint — **Iran's remaining leverage *is* the strait, and every strike that further empties it erodes the value of the asset Iran is holding hostage.** That is a weak limiter and it is weakening.

**Today's development is a new category, not a broken limiter.** Until now the belligerent attacking merchant shipping was Iran. **The US has now disabled a neutral-flag merchant hull by missile.** No one died, and CENTCOM's stated rationale — blockade enforcement after ignored warnings — is a lawful-belligerent framing rather than an attack on commerce as such.

**What it changes for the board:** it makes near-term reopening **less** likely, not more. **A blockade enforced by Hellfire against neutral-flag commercial traffic requires a political decision to lift, not a technical arrangement.** The Iran–Oman coordinate track was always a technical fix; it cannot dissolve a US naval interdiction that is now demonstrably shoot-capable.

**What would break the current equilibrium, concretely:** a strike that kills crew on a US-interdicted vessel, or an Iranian attack on a Gulf oil *field* or stabilisation train rather than shipping. Neither happened today.

## 4. The Economic / Sanctions Front

**Iranian barrels move at a $12–$15 discount**, with Chinese teapots taking **~1.5–1.8 mb/d**, often outside USD channels. **Russian Urals to China ~$7 under**, near the widest since 2022 — Russia and Iran are in a discount war over a capped pool of independent-refiner demand while Chinese state firms avoid sanctioned supply.

**Two source conflicts I am flagging rather than resolving in the convenient direction:**

1. One report states **"Iran has shipped more than 40 million barrels since the U.S. lifted its naval blockade."** That cannot describe the present — the blockade was being enforced by Hellfire missile yesterday. It most likely refers to the June truce window. **I am not treating it as current export data.**
2. A July 6 report says China **"has not been particularly enthusiastic about purchasing Iranian oil."** That sits directly against the 1.5–1.8 mb/d teapot flow this registry logged on Aug 10. **Both cannot be fully right.** The teapot figure is better sourced and more recent so it stands, but the disagreement is on the record.

**Enforcement credibility just changed, and in one direction only.** For months the sanctions architecture produced a discount, not a volume loss. **A missile through a container ship's steering gear is a different instrument.** If the US sustains kinetic interdiction, the binding constraint on Iranian exports shifts from *price* to *physical access* — and that is materially more bullish than the sanctions regime has been.

**The quota-versus-delivery gap.** Reporting puts the group **~7.51 mb/d below its collective target in June** — attributed, not asserted, since it depends on which members and baseline are counted. Better established: **four straight months of quota increases have not produced proportional supply growth.** Iraq and Kazakhstan already pump above their final August quotas; Russia runs below on aging and drone-damaged infrastructure.

**And the specifically 2026 problem: raising Saudi quota does not help if the barrels cannot leave.** Saudi's incremental crude loads on the Gulf coast — into a strait running six vessels a day.

**A caveat on spare capacity that cuts against my own bull framing:** the IEA and Energy Intelligence note OPEC holds *more* spare crude capacity than ever after years of restraint — **but the bulk of it sits with Mideast Gulf producers whose exports are themselves susceptible to disruption if the war escalates.** So the shock absorber exists on paper and sits behind the same chokepoint that would need it. That is worse than having none, because it invites complacency.

**Combined ≈ 8.8 mb/d nameplate against ~20 mb/d of normal Hormuz flow. Under half, with everything working.**

***"Hormuz bypass infrastructure was sized for a short disruption. This is not that."*** Petroline and ADCOP were insurance against a weeks-long closure. We are in month six.

**New risk to the bypass, from today's insurance data:** the Joint War Committee's northward extension of the Red Sea listed area now puts **Yanbu** — Petroline's outlet — inside a higher-premium zone. **The workaround route is starting to acquire the same insurance problem as the route it works around.** That is the first sign of the bypass thesis degrading from the demand side rather than the construction side.

**The clock:** every month the strait stays shut, more Gulf export capability migrates to routes Iran cannot reach. The UAE line lands **mid-2027** — roughly **eighteen months of runway** on a bull thesis built on closure. Iran can read the same construction schedules, which argues for escalating sooner rather than later.
