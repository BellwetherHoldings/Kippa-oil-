## 3. The Escalation Limiter — named today, and it is fraying

**What is holding:** both sides have largely spared each other's oil **production and processing** assets while freely attacking **shipping** and **military** targets. That is the entire reason this is a *transit* crisis and not a *supply-destruction* crisis. It is not a diplomatic achievement — there is no agreement covering it. It is mutual deterrence.

**The deterrent is explicit and documented.** A senior Iranian security official told IRGC-affiliated Tasnim on **July 30** that Iran has prepared an attack plan covering **regional energy infrastructure — oil fields and energy sites in Israel, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain and the UAE** — framed as a response to reported US/Israeli plans to strike **Iranian** energy infrastructure. Trump has separately threatened Iran's energy infrastructure absent a ceasefire. Each side is holding the other's production hostage.

**What breaks it — and how close we are:**

- **Aug 8, ADNOC tankers.** Iran struck the shipping of a Gulf neighbour's *national oil company*. One step short: the vessels, not the fields.
- **Aug 9, Jazan refinery.** A Houthi drone hit *onshore Saudi processing*. One step short: fire extinguished, no injuries, no throughput loss reported.

**Assessment: FRAYING, NOT BROKEN.** I am not raising severity on this. It is a statement of what the current 8 already prices and what a 9 or 10 would look like — recorded now so that a strike on a Gulf oil field or a Saudi stabilisation train is recognised immediately as a **regime change**, not as one more headline in a long series. **This is the largest unpriced risk on the board.**

## 4. The Economic / Sanctions Front

**Iran is selling, and enforcement is not stopping it.** Iranian crude moves at a **$12–$15 discount**, with China's independent "teapot" refiners absorbing **~1.5–1.8 mb/d**, frequently settled outside USD banking channels. This is the mechanism that keeps the global barrel count liquid despite the sanctions architecture, and it is why the price response to Iranian supply news is consistently smaller than the headlines imply.

**Russia is competing for the same buyer, and losing on price.** Urals to China has widened to about **$7/bbl** under, near the widest since 2022, as Russia's western-port shipments hit record highs — Moscow is pushing crude exports precisely *because* the drone campaign is destroying its domestic refining. **Russia and Iran are now in a discount war over shrinking Chinese teapot quota.** Chinese state firms continue to avoid sanctioned supply, so the entire contest is over a capped pool of independent-refiner demand.

**Is enforcement credible? On this evidence, no.** A sanctions regime that produces a $12–15 discount and 1.5 mb/d of steady flow is repricing the barrel, not removing it. The `iran-sanctions-waiver-expiry-2026` event contributes **0.31** to the risk score, and that contribution is best understood as pricing *friction and discount*, not *volume loss*.

## 5. OPEC+ — the counterweight, and the gap that guts it

The August 2 meeting of the seven (Saudi, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, Oman) raised September quotas **+188 kb/d**, the sixth consecutive monthly increase, **completing the phased rollback of the 2023 voluntary cuts.** Delegates expect quotas **flat for the rest of 2026** thereafter.

**A source contradiction, not smoothed over:** one outlet headlined this as OPEC+ agreeing to **cut** 188 kb/d. Four others — Rigzone, World Oil, Energy Connects, and a piece titled "188,000 bpd Increase, Not a Cut" — say **increase**, consistent with the sixth-consecutive-hike framing and with quota *levels* rising. **Treated as an increase.** The disagreement is recorded.

**The quota-versus-delivery gap is the whole story, and it cuts the bear case in half.** Reporting puts the group **~7.51 mb/d below its collective target in June 2026** — a figure I am attributing rather than asserting, since it depends heavily on which members and which baseline are counted. What is better established: **four consecutive months of quota increases have not produced proportional supply growth.** Iraq and Kazakhstan are already pumping **above** their final August quotas, so their "increases" deliver no new barrels. Russia is running below quota on aging and drone-damaged infrastructure.

**And the specifically 2026 problem: raising Saudi quota does not help if the barrels cannot leave.** Saudi's incremental crude loads on the Gulf coast — into a strait at 15% capacity. A quota increase and a delivered barrel are different objects, and this month they are further apart than at any point in the crisis.

**The second-order effect is the one nobody is pricing.** With cuts fully unwound, roughly **2 mb/d of paper spare capacity has been converted into flowing barrels.** That is bearish on price today and **bearish on the market's shock absorber tomorrow**: OPEC+'s ability to answer a further Hormuz deterioration by opening taps is materially smaller than it was in June. The group has spent its ammunition into a crisis that has not peaked.

## Freight & Insurance — and why the two measures disagree

| Metric | Pre-war | **Now** |
|---|---|---|

| Multiple vs pre-crisis | 1× | **30–40×** |

| Premium, 270,000 dwt VLCC, peak | — | **~$21M** |
| VLCC spot day-rate, peak fixtures | ~$20,000 | **>$770,000** |
| P&I clubs withdrawn | 0 | **6** |

**The 30× figure this platform logged on Aug 9 is corroborated.** Independent reporting now puts Hormuz war-risk at **7.5–10% of hull value** against a **~0.25%** pre-escalation Gulf baseline. That is 30–40×. Our registry entry was right.

**Now the important part — this explains the contradiction I have been flagging for three days.**

There is a published economic threshold: **at ~2% war-risk premium, routing through Hormuz and routing around the Cape of Good Hope cost about the same per voyage. At 3% or above, the Cape is cheaper despite 21 extra days at sea.**

**We are at 7.5–10%. The Cape is not marginally cheaper — it is decisively cheaper.**

**Therefore the collapse in Hormuz vessel counts is substantially a commercial decision, not a physical blockade.** Owners are not being stopped; they are declining to go, because the insurance makes the short route the expensive one. That distinction matters enormously for the bull thesis:

- If transit is **physically blocked**, reopening requires a military or diplomatic resolution.
- If transit is **priced out**, it reopens the moment underwriters reprice — which can happen in days, without any political agreement, on nothing more than a quiet fortnight.

**And it dissolves the vessel-count-versus-volume disagreement.** Ships that do transit are increasingly the largest, most economically compelling cargoes — a VLCC can absorb a $21M premium across 2 million barrels ($10.50/bbl) far better than a small product tanker can. **So vessel counts fall faster than volumes.** Both measures are correct; they are measuring different things.
