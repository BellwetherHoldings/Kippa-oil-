## 3. CORRECTION — I Named an Escalation Limiter Yesterday That Does Not Exist

**Yesterday I wrote that both sides had "largely spared each other's oil production and processing while attacking shipping and military targets," assessed that restraint as "FRAYING, NOT BROKEN," and called it "the largest unpriced risk on the board." That was wrong. The limiter has not existed since March 2026.**

The record I should have checked:

- **Mar 18–19** — Israeli drone strike damages four gas-treatment plants at Iran's **Asaluyeh** complex serving South Pars.
- **Mar 19–20** — Iran retaliates within hours against **Ras Laffan**, the heart of Qatar's energy sector. Shell's **Pearl GTL**, the world's largest gas-to-liquids plant, is damaged. A second strike takes out **17% of Qatar's LNG capacity, with repairs projected at three to five years.**
- **Mar 19–20** — Iranian drones and missiles breach perimeters at Kuwait's **Mina Al-Ahmadi** and **Mina Abdullah** refineries, firing multiple units. **Apr 3**, Mina Al-Ahmadi is hit again. Israel's **Haifa** refinery struck Mar 19.
- **Total Gulf energy damage: $58 billion.**

**What I actually observed was that the current phase — since fighting resumed in July — has concentrated on shipping. A phase is not a limiter.**

**And this is the same error for the third time in a week.** On Aug 4 I read twelve quiet days at Bab el-Mandeb as reduced Houthi intent and cut severity; the blockade resumed within a day. On Aug 4 I also read a draft clause as the crisis's first real concession; it inverted into a 7% cargo fee. Now I have read a few weeks of shipping-focused targeting as a standing mutual restraint. **In all three cases I treated a short quiet sample as evidence about intent.** That is a systematic bias in how I read this conflict, not three unrelated slips.

**Consequence for the board — and it runs opposite to yesterday's framing.** The upside tail is **fatter**, not thinner. Strikes on Gulf energy infrastructure are not an unbroken taboo whose breach would constitute a regime change; they are a **demonstrated, repeatedly used part of Iran's retaliation playbook**, already responsible for LNG capacity that stays offline for years. Anyone who read yesterday's report and concluded "the big risk is still ahead of us, and we'd see it coming" got the wrong picture from me.

**Severity held at 8.** This corrects an interpretation, not a fact. Nothing new was struck today.

**What is actually restraining escalation, as best I can tell:** not a taboo on energy targets, but the fact that Iran's remaining leverage *is* the strait — and each strike that further empties it also further erodes the value of the asset Iran is holding hostage. That is an economic limiter, not a moral one, and it is much weaker.

## 4. The Economic / Sanctions Front

**Iran is selling and enforcement is not stopping it.** Iranian crude moves at a **$12–$15 discount**, with China's independent "teapot" refiners taking **~1.5–1.8 mb/d**, frequently settled outside USD banking channels. This is why price responses to Iranian supply headlines keep undershooting the rhetoric — the barrels are repriced, not removed.

**Russia is competing for the same buyer and losing on price.** Urals to China has widened to about **$7/bbl** under, near the widest since 2022, as Russia pushes crude exports precisely *because* the drone campaign is destroying its domestic refining. **Russia and Iran are in a discount war over a capped pool of teapot quota** — Chinese state firms still avoid sanctioned supply.

**Credibility verdict: low.** A regime producing a $12–15 discount and 1.5 mb/d of steady flow is taxing the barrel, not removing it. The `iran-sanctions-waiver-expiry-2026` contribution of **0.31** should be read as pricing *friction and discount*, not volume loss.

**New this week: the US blockade is the harder constraint.** Trump describes an "infallible" blockade of Iranian ports — a "steel wall" where no vessel enters or leaves without US clearance, and specifically *"we do not let them go into Iran."* If that holds physically, it does more to Iranian exports than the sanctions architecture has managed in years. **Unverified independently; logged as a claim, not a fact.**

Corroborated again today: premiums went from **1–3% only weeks earlier to 7.5–10% now**, with underwriters showing **less appetite to provide spot coverage at all**.

**The threshold that explains everything: at ~2% premium, Hormuz and the Cape of Good Hope cost about the same per voyage. At 3%+, the Cape is cheaper despite 21 extra days at sea. We are at 7.5–10%.**

**So the closure is substantially a commercial decision, not a physical blockade** — and today Trump inadvertently confirmed the mechanism by claiming the strait is militarily controlled and "actually open" on the same day it ran six vessels. Owners are not being stopped. They are declining to go.

**Why vessel counts fall faster than volumes:** a VLCC absorbs a $21M premium across 2 million barrels — about **$10.50/bbl**. A small product tanker cannot. So the transits that survive are the largest, most economically compelling cargoes, and **counts collapse faster than tonnage.** Both measures are honest; they measure different things.
